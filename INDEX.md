# Complete Index of 2007

A comprehensive index of all 63 notable events, people, works, and developments of 2007, organized by category and date.

2007 was a hinge year: the consumer technology revolution reached a mass tipping point — Apple sold 270,000 iPhones over the device's opening weekend, and Facebook opened its platform to outside developers in May — while the financial system that had underpinned years of growth quietly began to buckle. Bear Stearns disclosed in July that two of its hedge funds had lost "essentially all their value," inflicting roughly $1.8 billion in investor losses and marking the moment the subprime mortgage crisis became a systemic event. Geopolitically, the year closed with Benazir Bhutto's assassination on December 27 — the first woman ever to lead a Muslim-majority country, killed on the campaign trail in Rawalpindi. These 63 entries document that convergence.

## Browse by Time Period

### Early 2007 (Jan–Mar)

The year opened with two defining announcements. On January 9, Steve Jobs took the stage at Macworld in San Francisco's Moscone Center and unveiled the iPhone — describing it as "a widescreen iPod with touch controls, a revolutionary mobile phone, and a breakthrough internet communicator." Apple's stock jumped more than 8 percent that day. Three days earlier, Nancy Pelosi had been sworn in as the first female Speaker of the House. On January 10, President Bush announced a troop surge of 21,500 additional soldiers to Iraq over opposition from the new Democratic-controlled Congress. February brought scientific alarm: the IPCC released its Fourth Assessment Report on February 2, the first to state unequivocally that warming of the climate system was "unequivocal" and attributable to human activity with 90 percent confidence. The same month, HSBC Holdings disclosed a $10.6 billion write-down on its U.S. subprime mortgage portfolio — the first major signal that subprime losses had spread to large international banks.

### Mid 2007 (Apr–Jun)

April 16 brought the deadliest campus shooting in U.S. history: Seung-Hui Cho killed 32 people — 27 students and 5 faculty — across two buildings at Virginia Tech in Blacksburg, Virginia, over roughly two hours before taking his own life. The massacre reopened national debate on gun access and mental health reporting gaps. In May, Facebook opened its platform to third-party developers, and in May Google launched Street View in five U.S. cities. The iPhone finally went on sale on June 29 at 6:00 p.m. local time, with lines stretching city blocks outside Apple stores; Apple sold 1 million units within 74 days of launch. Beneath these headlines, the first cracks in the credit system were widening: New Century Financial, the nation's largest subprime lender, had filed for bankruptcy in April, and by late June Bear Stearns was pledging $3.2 billion of its own capital to prevent one of its hedge funds from collapsing entirely.

### Summer 2007 (Jul–Sep)

The financial rupture arrived in July. On July 16, Bear Stearns disclosed that both its High-Grade Structured Credit Strategies funds had lost essentially all their value, leaving investors with approximately $1.8 billion in losses. On August 9, BNP Paribas froze three investment funds worth €2 billion, citing an inability to value U.S. mortgage-backed securities — triggering a global interbank freeze. The European Central Bank injected €94.8 billion into money markets that day, its largest single-day liquidity operation in history. In the UK, the crisis produced a visual shock: on September 14, depositors queued outside Northern Rock branches across England in the first British bank run in 150 years, withdrawing an estimated £1 billion within days. Against this backdrop, cultural life produced some of its decade-defining moments: Harry Potter and the Deathly Hallows sold 11 million copies in its first 24 hours (July 21), Live Earth concerts drew 2 billion viewers across 7 continents (July 7), and on August 7 Barry Bonds hit home run number 756 to surpass Hank Aaron's all-time record. On September 16, Arctic sea ice extent reached a record low of 4.13 million km², 23 percent below the previous record.

### Fall 2007 (Oct–Dec)

The Dow Jones Industrial Average peaked at 14,164.53 on October 9 — its highest close in history and the high-water mark before the 2008 collapse — even as oil approached $100 per barrel and credit conditions tightened. October 10 brought an unusual Nobel Peace Prize: Al Gore and the IPCC shared the award for climate-change communication, six days after An Inconvenient Truth had appeared in 1,000 U.S. theaters. On November 19, Amazon launched the Kindle at $399, selling out within hours — a device that would redefine publishing. The following day, November 20, Shinya Yamanaka's lab at Kyoto University and James Thomson's group at the University of Wisconsin simultaneously published papers showing that ordinary adult skin cells could be reprogrammed into induced pluripotent stem cells using just four transcription factors, bypassing the ethical barriers to embryonic stem cell research that had paralyzed the field for years. Yamanaka would share the 2012 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the discovery. The year closed violently: Benazir Bhutto, the first woman ever to lead a Muslim-majority country and Pakistan's most prominent democratic opposition figure, was assassinated at a Rawalpindi political rally on December 27, killing 23 others and triggering protests that left dozens more dead.

## Key Themes of 2007

### The Digital Inflection Point

2007 was arguably the most concentrated single year of consumer-technology transformation since the introduction of the personal computer. The iPhone (January 9 announcement, June 29 launch) established the multi-touch smartphone form factor that would make every prior smartphone paradigm — stylus-driven Windows Mobile, Nokia's Symbian — obsolete within five years. The Facebook Platform (May 24) opened the social graph to outside developers, enabling the mobile-social media convergence that would define the next decade. Google Street View (May 25), YouTube's explosive growth as a mass-media platform, the Amazon Kindle (November 19), and the conception of Singularity University all occurred within this single calendar year. The parallel scientific revolution — Yamanaka and Thomson's induced pluripotent stem cell breakthrough (November 20–21) — demonstrated that 2007's transformation was not confined to consumer electronics.

### The Prelude to 2008: Financial Fragility

Every major financial stress marker of 2008 was already visible in 2007. The subprime mortgage crisis crystallized in July when Bear Stearns disclosed $1.8 billion in hedge-fund losses; the interbank market froze in August; Northern Rock became the first British bank run in 150 years in September; oil prices climbed toward $100 per barrel; and the stock market peaked at 14,164 on October 9 — its last all-time high until 2013. The Hank Paulson Treasury's ARM rate-freeze plan and Ben Bernanke's Federal Reserve rate cuts were reactive measures to a crisis already structural. The year's financial arc is the essential context for understanding how 2008's collapse was neither sudden nor unpredicted.

### Democratic Struggles and Political Violence

Democracy faced organized violence across three continents in 2007. Myanmar's Buddhist monks led the Saffron Uprising in September before it was suppressed by the military junta. Benazir Bhutto survived a suicide bombing that killed 139 people on her return to Pakistan in October, then was assassinated in December. Darfur's conflict reached its most internationally visible phase. Pakistan's President Musharraf suspended the constitution in November. In contrast, Gordon Brown's assumption of the UK prime ministership in June and the Australian Labor Party's November election victory represented peaceful democratic transitions — while in the U.S., Nancy Pelosi's January speakership marked a historic shift in congressional power that immediately constrained Bush's Iraq policy.

### Cultural Closures and Openings

Several of 2007's most-discussed cultural events were endings: The Sopranos concluded its eight-year run on June 10 with a deliberately ambiguous cut to black; Harry Potter and the Deathly Hallows ended the seven-book series on July 21. Radiohead's In Rainbows (October 10), released on a pay-what-you-want basis, opened a new question about how music could be distributed and valued in a post-CD world. The Writers Guild of America strike (November 5) forced the television industry to confront changing production economics. These moments — closures of long narrative arcs, experiments with new models — capture a cultural system in transition alongside the technological one.
