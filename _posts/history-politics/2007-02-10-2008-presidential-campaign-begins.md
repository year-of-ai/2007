## Summary

The 2008 U.S. presidential campaign began in earnest in early 2007 with a wave of major candidate announcements. Hillary Clinton, U.S. Senator from New York and former First Lady, announced her candidacy via a YouTube video on January 20, 2007, with the phrase "I'm in, and I'm in to win"—positioning herself as the Democratic frontrunner backed by the Clinton fundraising network and near-universal name recognition. On February 10, 2007, Barack Hussein Obama, the junior U.S. Senator from Illinois elected only in 2004, announced his candidacy before an estimated crowd of 17,000 at the Old State Capitol in Springfield, Illinois—the same venue where Abraham Lincoln had declared the nation "a house divided" in 1858. Obama's choice of Springfield was deliberate, evoking a transformative historical moment to frame his candidacy as a generational break from partisan polarization.

The Democratic primary field eventually encompassed eight major candidates: Clinton, Obama, John Edwards (2004 VP nominee and 2008 policy-focused progressive), Joseph Biden (Senate Foreign Relations Committee chairman), Christopher Dodd, Mike Gravel, Dennis Kucinich, and Bill Richardson (the only Latino candidate with executive experience as New Mexico's governor). On the Republican side, Rudy Giuliani entered the race on February 5, 2007, banking on his post-9/11 national security image and led national polls for much of the year despite social moderate positions that alienated the evangelical base. John McCain formally announced his candidacy on April 25, 2007, at an event in Portsmouth, New Hampshire, struggling early but ultimately prevailing after a dramatic comeback from near-bankruptcy in summer 2007. Mike Huckabee entered July 30, 2007; Mitt Romney announced his campaign at the Henry Ford Museum in Dearborn, Michigan on February 13, 2007, emphasizing business credentials from his Bain Capital career and the turnaround of the 2002 Salt Lake City Winter Olympics.

The 2007 campaign unfolded against a backdrop of profound national anxiety. The [Iraq War troop surge]({{ '/news/history-politics/iraq-war-troop-surge/' | relative_url }})—announced by President Bush on January 10, 2007, deploying an additional 21,500 U.S. troops—dominated debate in both primaries. Democrats who had voted to authorize the 2003 invasion, including Clinton and Edwards, faced sustained pressure to account for that vote, while Obama's consistent opposition to the war from 2002 onward became central to his contrast narrative. Simultaneously, early tremors of the subprime mortgage crisis—which would explode into full financial collapse by September 2008—created economic unease that shaped voters' appetite for change. [Nancy Pelosi's ascent to Speaker of the House]({{ '/news/history-politics/nancy-pelosi-speaker/' | relative_url }}) in January 2007 and the Democratic congressional majorities elected in 2006 sharpened expectations that the Bush era's policies would be repudiated.

## 2007 Campaign Timeline

Key moments that shaped the race's trajectory during its first year:

- **January 20, 2007** — Hillary Clinton announces via YouTube: "I'm in, and I'm in to win." Her campaign launches with a reported $10 million first-quarter fundraising target.
- **January 31, 2007** — Joseph Biden's campaign begins inauspiciously after controversial remarks about Obama's articulateness in a New York Observer interview, forcing an apology within hours of launch.
- **February 5, 2007** — Rudy Giuliani files a presidential exploratory committee; enters the Republican race officially February 5, 2007.
- **February 10, 2007** — Barack Obama announces at Old State Capitol, Springfield, Illinois.
- **February 13, 2007** — Mitt Romney formally announces at the Henry Ford Museum, Dearborn, Michigan.
- **April 25, 2007** — John McCain announces candidacy in Portsmouth, New Hampshire; his campaign is simultaneously rocked by fundraising shortfalls.
- **April 26, 2007** — First major Democratic primary debate in Orangeburg, South Carolina (MSNBC/NBC), featuring all eight candidates.
- **June 2007** — Q2 fundraising reports reveal Obama raised $32.5 million to Clinton's $27 million—the first concrete signal that the race would be genuinely competitive.
- **July 23, 2007** — CNN/YouTube Democratic debate, the first presidential debate to incorporate citizen-submitted video questions, emphasizing digital politics.
- **July 2007** — McCain campaign nearly collapses: the campaign reports only $2 million cash on hand, lays off staff, and is widely written off.
- **August 11, 2007** — Obama draws 24,000 to a campaign rally in Ames, Iowa—then his largest crowd—underscoring the energy gap between his and Clinton's operations.
- **October 30, 2007** — Clinton stumbles in a Philadelphia debate when she gives inconsistent answers about New York Governor Eliot Spitzer's proposal to grant driver's licenses to undocumented immigrants; rival campaigns cite it as her first visible weakness.
- **November 2007** — National Democratic polls consistently show Clinton leading Obama 45–27%; Iowa polling shows a much tighter race that foreshadows her eventual defeat there.
- **December 2007** — Oprah Winfrey campaigns for Obama in Iowa and South Carolina—the largest celebrity endorsement of the cycle—drawing crowds of 18,000 in Des Moines and 29,000 in Columbia.

## Fundraising and the Digital Revolution

The 2007 fundraising totals shattered prior records and signaled a structural shift in campaign finance. Obama raised an unprecedented $24.8 million in Q1 2007—compared to Clinton's $26 million, both far exceeding earlier expectations—with Obama drawing contributions from 104,000 donors, 90% of them online, at an average contribution of $200. By year-end, both candidates had raised over $100 million for the primary season, levels previously associated only with general elections under the old public-finance system.

Obama's campaign, managed by campaign manager David Plouffe and chief strategist David Axelrod, pioneered the use of email list-building, microtargeting, and social-network organizing (through the in-house platform MyBarackObama.com, which registered over 1.5 million users) to mobilize first-time donors. The campaign's decision to forgo public financing in the general election—a first for a major-party nominee—enabled Obama to raise $745 million total, nearly double the $367 million raised by John McCain (who accepted public financing and its $84 million spending cap for the general election).

This fundraising revolution had lasting structural effects: it ended public financing as a viable general-election strategy, established small-dollar online fundraising as the primary Democratic party infrastructure, and shifted power from traditional party bundlers toward digital-native organizing networks.

## The Republican Primary

The Republican field faced a different structural challenge: no incumbent president or sitting vice president was running for the first time since 1952. Bush's approval ratings (around 30% in 2007) created headwinds for any candidate promising continuity, yet primary voters remained largely conservative on social issues—creating a difficult needle to thread for candidates like Giuliani (pro-choice, pro-gun-control) and Romney (who had governed Massachusetts as a moderate).

Giuliani's early dominance in national polls (consistently 30%+) reflected name recognition rather than deep ideological affinity. His strategy of skipping the early states (Iowa, New Hampshire, South Carolina) to compete in Florida's January 29 primary proved fatal—by the time Florida voted, McCain had won New Hampshire (January 8, 2008) and Huckabee had won Iowa (January 3, 2008), producing momentum that collapsed Giuliani's support. Romney, despite $44.6 million in personal loans to his campaign, could not consolidate conservative support against Huckabee in Iowa. McCain's comeback—fueled by endorsements from the New Hampshire Union Leader and veteran military figures—set up his general election matchup against Obama.

The [Scooter Libby commutation]({{ '/news/history-politics/scooter-libby-commutation/' | relative_url }}) by President Bush in July 2007 and the resignation of Attorney General Alberto Gonzales in August 2007 (amid the U.S. Attorneys firing scandal) further eroded Republican voter enthusiasm and deepened the field's need to distinguish itself from Bush-era policies.

## Historical Significance

The 2008 presidential race represented a historic inflection point in American politics. The Democratic primary's two frontrunners—Hillary Clinton and Barack Obama—made unprecedented history: Clinton was the first woman to be a major-party presidential frontrunner with a credible path to nomination, while Obama was the first African-American candidate to win a major-party presidential nomination and to win a general election. Obama's ultimate victory on November 4, 2008, with 52.9% of the popular vote and 365 Electoral College votes to McCain's 173, elected the nation's 44th president and its first African-American head of state at age 47.

The campaign also demonstrated the limits of the traditional Democratic "inevitability" strategy—the assumption that Clinton's organizational and financial advantages would produce a quick consolidation of the nomination. Obama's Iowa caucus victory on January 3, 2008 (with 37.6% of delegates to Clinton's 29.5%) shattered that assumption and proved that a candidate without Clinton's institutional advantages could win if they built superior grassroots infrastructure in the early states.

For Al Gore, who had won the [Nobel Peace Prize in October 2007]({{ '/news/history-politics/al-gore-ipcc-nobel-peace-prize/' | relative_url }}) for his climate activism, the 2008 cycle illustrated an alternative path of post-political influence: rather than running for president a second time (as many supporters urged), Gore used the Nobel platform to embed climate change as a mainstream Democratic priority—a framing Obama's campaign subsequently adopted.

The campaign cycle pioneered digital-first political strategy that became the template for all subsequent cycles: social media mobilization, viral video, online fundraising at scale, data-driven microtargeting, and distributed volunteer organizing via web platforms. Patterns established in 2007–08 have defined every presidential cycle since.
